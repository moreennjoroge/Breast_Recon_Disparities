# Scientific Writing Best Practices for Plastic Surgery Journals

## Writing Clarity and Style

### General Principles
- Use clear, concise, professional medical English
- Avoid jargon unless standard in the field
- Use active voice when possible (e.g., "We performed" rather than "It was performed")
- Be specific rather than vague
- Maintain objectivity and balance

### Sentence Structure
- Keep sentences straightforward—one main idea per sentence
- Vary sentence length for readability
- Avoid overly complex nested clauses
- Use parallel structure for lists
- Front-load important information

### Paragraph Organization
- Begin each paragraph with topic sentence
- One main idea per paragraph
- Use transition words between ideas
- End paragraphs with concluding or bridging statement
- Keep paragraphs focused (4-6 sentences typically)

## Section-Specific Writing Guidelines

### Introduction Writing
**Purpose**: Establish need for study and state objectives

**Structure:**
1. Opening statement: Establish clinical significance
2. Background: Brief literature review showing knowledge gap
3. Study objective: Clear statement of what this study addresses
4. Brief preview: One sentence on approach

**Tips:**
- Start broad, then narrow to specific research question
- Avoid extensive historical review
- Cite only most relevant, recent literature
- Make the "gap" explicit
- End with clear objective or hypothesis

**Example opening pattern:**
"[Procedure/condition] remains a significant challenge in plastic surgery, affecting [number] patients annually. While [established approaches] have shown [results], [limitation or gap] persists. This study aimed to [specific objective] by [approach]."

### Methods Writing
**Purpose**: Enable replication and demonstrate rigor

**Key elements:**
- Study design clearly stated upfront
- Patient population well-defined
- Procedures described with sufficient detail
- Outcome measures operationally defined
- Statistical approach justified

**Tips:**
- Use past tense throughout
- Be specific with numbers (e.g., exact follow-up duration, sample size)
- Define all abbreviations on first use
- Include model/brand names for equipment when relevant
- State inclusion/exclusion criteria explicitly

**Example patterns:**
- "A retrospective cohort study was conducted at [institution] from [dates]."
- "Patients were included if they underwent [procedure] and had minimum [time] follow-up."
- "The primary outcome was [measure], defined as [operational definition]."
- "Statistical significance was set at α=0.05. All analyses were performed using [software]."

### Results Writing
**Purpose**: Present findings objectively without interpretation

**Structure:**
1. Patient demographics and baseline characteristics
2. Primary outcome results
3. Secondary outcome results
4. Complications or adverse events
5. Subgroup analyses (if performed)

**Tips:**
- State findings directly—no hedging in Results
- Use past tense for completed actions, present tense for data in tables/figures
- Present actual numbers alongside percentages
- Reference tables/figures to support text
- Include measures of precision (CI, standard deviation)
- Avoid "trends" or "tendencies"—report actual statistics

**Example patterns:**
- "The mean age was 42.3 years (SD=11.2, range 21-67)."
- "The complication rate was 12% (18/150 patients), including [list complications]."
- "Patients in Group A showed significantly greater improvement than Group B (mean difference 2.3 points, 95% CI 1.1-3.5; p=0.001)."
- "Patient satisfaction scores improved from baseline to 6 months postoperatively (7.2±1.1 vs 8.9±0.8; p<0.001)."

### Discussion Writing
**Purpose**: Interpret findings and place in context

**Structure:**
1. Restatement of principal findings (1 paragraph)
2. Comparison with existing literature (2-3 paragraphs)
3. Explanation of findings/mechanisms (1-2 paragraphs)
4. Clinical implications (1-2 paragraphs)
5. Limitations (1 paragraph)
6. Future directions (optional, 1 paragraph)
7. Conclusion (1 paragraph)

**Tips:**
- Do not repeat detailed results
- Compare findings to similar studies
- Address contradictions with previous literature
- Be honest about limitations
- Avoid overstating conclusions
- Use present tense for general statements, past tense for study-specific findings
- Balance enthusiasm with scientific objectivity

**Example patterns:**
- "This study demonstrates that [main finding]. This finding is consistent with [previous study], which reported [similar finding]."
- "The improvement in [outcome] may be attributed to [mechanism or explanation]."
- "These findings suggest that [clinical implication], potentially benefiting [patient population]."
- "This study has several limitations. First, [limitation]. Second, [limitation]."
- "In conclusion, [restate main finding] and [clinical implication]."

## Effective Data Presentation

### When to Use Tables vs Figures vs Text

**Use tables for:**
- Patient demographics
- Multiple outcome measures
- Comparative data across groups
- Statistical results (odds ratios, confidence intervals)
- Classification systems or staging

**Use figures for:**
- Before/after photographs
- Surgical technique illustrations
- Trends over time (line graphs)
- Proportions (pie charts, bar graphs)
- Anatomical drawings
- Flowcharts (e.g., patient selection)

**Use text for:**
- Single values or simple comparisons
- Narrative descriptions
- Information that doesn't require visualization

### Figure Captions
- Begin with brief descriptive phrase
- Define all abbreviations used in figure
- Note any statistical significance markers (*, †)
- Include patient consent statement for clinical photos
- State scale or magnification for microscopy

**Example:** "Preoperative (left) and 6-month postoperative (right) views demonstrating improved contour and symmetry following autologous fat grafting. Patient provided informed consent for publication."

### Table Construction
- Title above table describing contents
- Column headers clear and concise
- Use consistent significant figures
- Include units of measurement
- Footnotes below for abbreviations
- Use horizontal lines sparingly—no vertical lines

## Common Phrases and Language Patterns

### Introducing Studies
- "To address this gap, we conducted..."
- "The purpose of this study was to..."
- "We hypothesized that..."
- "This study aimed to determine whether..."

### Presenting Results
- "Patients who underwent [X] demonstrated..."
- "The mean [outcome] was significantly higher/lower in..."
- "No significant difference was observed between..."
- "Multivariate analysis revealed that..."

### Discussing Findings
- "These results support the hypothesis that..."
- "Our findings are consistent with previous reports..."
- "One possible explanation for this finding is..."
- "The clinical significance of this finding is..."
- "Contrary to our hypothesis..."
- "This study has several strengths, including..."

### Acknowledging Limitations
- "This study has several limitations."
- "The retrospective design limits..."
- "The relatively small sample size..."
- "Selection bias may have influenced..."
- "Residual confounding cannot be excluded..."

### Stating Conclusions
- "In conclusion, this study demonstrates that..."
- "These findings suggest that [procedure] is safe and effective for..."
- "Further research is needed to..."
- "This approach offers a promising alternative to..."

## Title Optimization

### Effective Titles
- Include study design (retrospective cohort, systematic review, etc.)
- Be specific about intervention and population
- Keep under 15-20 words when possible
- Use colons to separate main title from subtitle
- Include key searchable terms

**Examples:**
- "Autologous Fat Grafting for Facial Rejuvenation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis"
- "Deep Inferior Epigastric Perforator Flap Breast Reconstruction: A 10-Year Retrospective Cohort Study"
- "Outcomes of Cleft Lip Repair in Low-Resource Settings: A Prospective Multicenter Study"

### Keywords Selection
- Use MeSH terms when possible
- Include procedure names
- Include anatomical sites
- Include condition/diagnosis
- Include study design type
- Typically 4-6 keywords

## Citation Best Practices

### When to Cite
- Previous studies on same topic
- Established techniques you use
- Statistical methods (if novel)
- Classification systems
- Definitions of terms
- Claims requiring support

### When Not to Cite
- Common knowledge in field
- Your own methods/techniques
- General statements
- Information from multiple sources (cite review instead)

### Citation Integration
- Integrate citations smoothly into text
- Cite at end of clause or sentence
- Group multiple citations in chronological order
- Cite original source, not secondary citation when possible

**Examples:**
- "Previous studies have shown that complications decrease with increased surgeon experience.^1-3"
- "The Manchester system was used to classify areolar scarring.^12"
- "As reported by Smith et al, the overall satisfaction rate exceeded 90%.^8"

## Revision Strategies

### Self-Editing Checklist
- [ ] Each paragraph has one clear main idea
- [ ] Sentences are varied in length
- [ ] Active voice used where appropriate
- [ ] Jargon minimized or defined
- [ ] Transitions between paragraphs clear
- [ ] Redundancy eliminated
- [ ] Abbreviations defined on first use
- [ ] Tense consistency maintained
- [ ] All claims supported by data or citations

### Common Errors to Fix
1. **Wordiness**: "Due to the fact that" → "Because"
2. **Weak verbs**: "Performed an analysis of" → "Analyzed"
3. **Nominalizations**: "Made the decision to" → "Decided"
4. **Redundancy**: "Past history" → "History"
5. **Vague quantifiers**: "Several patients" → "12 patients (15%)"
6. **Hedging in Results**: "Appeared to show" → "Showed"
7. **Opinion in Results**: "Unfortunately, 3 patients..." → "Three patients..."

### Improving Clarity
- Replace complex phrases with simple alternatives
- Break long sentences into shorter ones
- Use specific rather than general terms
- Define technical terms for broader audience
- Remove unnecessary modifiers
- Eliminate redundant phrases

## Professional Tone

### Appropriate Language
- Objective and balanced
- Confident but not arrogant
- Acknowledging of limitations
- Respectful of previous work
- Evidence-based claims

### Inappropriate Language
- Emotive language ("surprisingly," "unfortunately")
- Absolute statements ("always," "never")
- Promotional language ("exciting," "groundbreaking")
- Subjective opinions without support
- Dismissive of other studies
- Overstated conclusions

### Discussing Limitations
**Good example:** "The retrospective design limits our ability to establish causation. Additionally, the single-center experience may not be generalizable to other settings."

**Poor example:** "Unfortunately, our study has some limitations that readers should be aware of before accepting our conclusions."

## Final Review Questions

Before submission, ask:
1. Does the title accurately reflect the content?
2. Does the abstract stand alone effectively?
3. Are objectives clearly stated?
4. Are methods reproducible?
5. Are results presented objectively?
6. Does discussion interpret without repeating?
7. Are limitations acknowledged?
8. Are figures/tables necessary and clear?
9. Is every reference cited in text?
10. Is language clear, concise, and professional?
